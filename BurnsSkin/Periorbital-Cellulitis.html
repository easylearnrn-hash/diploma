👁️ Periorbital Cellulitis

NCLEX-RN High-Yield Notes

1. Definition

Periorbital Cellulitis (also: Preseptal Cellulitis) = infection of the eyelid and surrounding skin in front of the orbital septum.

Serious but usually not vision-threatening if treated promptly. Most common in children under 10.

2. Periorbital vs. Orbital Cellulitis

FeaturePeriorbital (Preseptal)Orbital (Postseptal)
LocationIn front of orbital septumBehind orbital septum (within orbit)
SeverityMore common; less dangerousRare; more severe, sight-threatening
Eye movement pain❌ Absent✅ Present
Vision changes❌ None✅ Possible
Proptosis (bulging eye)❌ No✅ Yes
UrgencyUrgent but often outpatientEmergency — needs IV antibiotics/hospital
Common causeSkin trauma, insect bite, sinusitisSinusitis progression, abscess
Pain with eye movement + proptosis = Orbital Cellulitis → EMERGENCY

3. Causes & Risk Factors

  • Sinusitis (especially ethmoid sinus)
  • Eyelid trauma (insect bite, scratch, injury)
  • Chalazion or stye infection
  • Spread from upper respiratory tract infection

Common Organisms

  • Staphylococcus aureus
  • Streptococcus pneumoniae
  • Haemophilus influenzae (unvaccinated children)

4. Signs & Symptoms

  • ✅ Eyelid swelling, redness, and tenderness
  • ✅ Warmth over the eyelid
  • ✅ Fever may be present
  • ✅ No pain with eye movement
  • ✅ No vision changes
  • ✅ Normal eye movements and pupil response
  • ✅ History of insect bite, sinus infection, or trauma

5. Diagnosis

  • Physical exam — distinguish from orbital cellulitis
  • CT scan of orbits with contrast (if uncertain or worsening)
  • CBC — elevated WBC indicates infection
  • Blood cultures if febrile or toxic-appearing

6. Treatment

SeverityTreatment
Mild (outpatient)Oral antibiotics: amoxicillin-clavulanate
Moderate/severe or age <1 yearIV antibiotics: ceftriaxone, vancomycin (hospital)
No improvement/complicationsSurgical drainage; ophthalmology referral

7. Nursing Care

  • Monitor temperature and vital signs regularly
  • Assess for progression to orbital cellulitis:
    • Pain with eye movement
    • Proptosis (bulging eye)
    • Diplopia (double vision)
    • Decreased visual acuity
  • Administer antibiotics on time
  • Warm compresses to eyelid (if approved)
  • Encourage medication compliance to prevent complications
  • Educate caregivers to return if symptoms worsen
Teach caregivers: eye pain, vision loss, or fever worsening = return to ER immediately.

8. NCLEX Practice Questions

A 6-year-old has eyelid swelling, redness, and warmth but no pain with eye movement and normal vision. Most likely diagnosis?
✅ C. Periorbital cellulitis — classic preseptal presentation
Which finding would cause the nurse to suspect orbital cellulitis over periorbital cellulitis?
✅ C. Pain with eye movement — indicates deeper orbital involvement
Most important discharge teaching for periorbital cellulitis?
✅ C. "Return if your child's eye becomes hard to open or moves painfully" — progression to orbital cellulitis

9. NCLEX Final Tips

  • Most common in children under 10
  • Differentiate: check eye movement pain and vision
  • Prompt antibiotics are key
  • Always educate caregivers on red flags: eye pain, vision loss, fever
  • CT scan may be ordered to rule out deeper infection
Memory trick:
Periorbital = in front = less dangerous = no eye movement pain
Orbital = behind septum = more dangerous = eye movement pain + proptosis
